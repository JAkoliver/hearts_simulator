## Abstract

We evaluate Perilune, a neural-network hearts engine (policy + belief + match-equity networks with optional determinized search), against Nathan Sturtevant's hearts program ("xinxin"), a Perfect-Information Monte Carlo + UCT engine, in xinxin's own game harness, in two sequentially preregistered studies. The design is duplicate-bridge style: every seed is played twice with engine seat assignments flipped (complement pairing), so deal luck cancels within pairs; compositions are stratified; each study's arms share one seed block, making deals identical across arms (common random numbers). All analyses, sample sizes, and instruments were frozen and registered before any counted run in each study.

**Study 1 (teacher configuration and budget brackets).** At shipped defaults (arm M-A: Perilune's searched *teacher* configuration vs. xinxin at sims = 10,000), Perilune is clearly stronger: paired mean placement difference −0.66 places/seat (95% CI [−0.98, −0.33], Holm-adjusted p = 0.0008, n = 24 pairs), match wins +0.83/pair (CI [+0.28, +1.39], q = 0.0048), winning 34 of 48 matches (tie-split). On the pure deal-skill axis (single-deal instrument, n = 156 paired deals), Perilune takes 2.00 points/deal fewer (CI [−2.99, −1.01]) and places 0.32 better (CI [−0.44, −0.20]). With search removed from both sides (M-B — a floor-setting ablation against xinxin's *rollout policy*), the scale saturates (−1.89 of a possible −2). When xinxin's budget is raised ~9× above his shipped default to 93,000 sims/decision — the wall-clock-parity point — the placement difference collapses to −0.08: a null reported as its 95% CI, [−0.62, +0.46] places, not as parity proven.

**Study 2 (the deployed product, properly powered).** Study 1's most product-relevant cell — Perilune v5's *raw* policy net (no search; the configuration the site served at the time of the study, run on CPU as deployed) against xinxin at his shipped default — was also its weakest (n = 8, unfavourable point estimates). Study 2 re-ran it as a confirmatory non-inferiority test at n = 438 pairs (876 matches), powered at the pilot SD's upper confidence limit, plus a five-level xinxin-budget frontier at n = 48/level (six times Study 1's 8/level). Result: **the raw net is STRONGER on placement** — −0.297 places/seat (CI [−0.38, −0.21], Holm q < 0.0001; exact Wilcoxon p < 0.0001) — and **not meaningfully worse on match wins** (−0.03/pair, CI [−0.16, +0.10], inside the ±0.5 registered margin), while spending **about one-sixth of xinxin's decision time per deal on the same processor** (452 vs. 2,685 ms). Under the registered dominance definition, the deployed product dominates xinxin's shipped default. On the frontier, the placement crossing (where xinxin's search would overtake the raw net) is right-censored beyond 93,000 sims (bootstrap 83% censored); on the win axis xinxin's search overtakes the raw net at ≈1,700 sims (bootstrap 95% [850, 13,400]) — the two engines' different objectives visible as two different curves. Descriptively, xinxin shoots the moon in ~15% of deals against the raw net — flat across the entire budget range, replicated across both studies' seed blocks, and roughly double his rate against the searched teacher on the same Study-1 seed block — so the raw net wins on placement while conceding a moon every seven deals; closing that hole is where the remaining margin lives.

Combined headline (all claims about **Perilune v5**, lineage 8a89da90, §1): **Perilune v5's raw policy net — the model deployed at the time of the study — beats xinxin's shipped default on placement, matches it on match wins, at a fraction of the cost on the same CPU; v5's searched teacher configuration beats it more decisively at defaults and on pure deal skill; at extreme xinxin budgets (≈200× the nominal playouts) the searched engine's match-level advantage is no longer detectable at Study 1's size, and the raw net's placement advantage shrinks toward — but in the tested range never crosses — zero.**

## 1. The opponent, and why this comparison

xinxin is Nathan Sturtevant's hearts program — to our knowledge the strongest classical open-source hearts engine, built on determinized (PIMC) UCT search with an opponent-model parameter. UCT-in-hearts was shown to beat the previous best programs in Sturtevant (2008); the program's lineage runs through his multi-player search work (Sturtevant 2003) and hearts learning experiments (Sturtevant & White 2007). It is also the baseline his own group chose most recently: Rebstock, Solinas, Sturtevant & Buro (2024) call it “the current state-of-the-art in computer Hearts AI” and evaluate a transformer-based approach (GO-MCTS) against it — the one prior published comparison against this opponent, and one that reports a *stronger* neural player than either configuration evaluated here, at the budget they tested (§S3). Nathan Sturtevant, xinxin’s author and a co-author of that paper, pointed us to it when we contacted him before releasing this report (§S3); notification is not review, and no endorsement by him or the paper’s other authors is implied. We evaluate against the program itself, pinned at commit `6ba11548` of `github.com/nathansttt/hearts` (GPL), with two recorded one-line local patches (a missing `<cassert>` include; enabling the `kLead2Clubs` rule flag) and rules mask 0xda1 chosen to match Perilune's training rules (Q♠ = 13, moon = 0/26/26/26, hearts break only on a heart, no points on trick 1, pass rotation L/R/A/H, `kQueenBreaksHearts` off).

**Model version.** This report evaluates one specific Perilune artifact: **Perilune v5** — the champion as of 2026-08-14/15, lineage id 8a89da90, consisting of the match-aware policy/search net `hearts_ai_search_match.pt` (md5 3a2abd364662d2759ace3f37f006394f, 556-input trace) and the equity net `hearts_equity.pt` (md5 efdfee07fbfd8cf97d5cbec8ea2dfd6f). The md5s are the ground truth of what was tested; "v5" and the lineage id are its human-readable handles, and every number in this report refers to this artifact and no other. Both md5s were re-verified unchanged before Study 1's pilot, at Study 2's registration, and before Study 2's first counted run. Results here are claims about Perilune v5 only — they should not be read as claims about later or earlier Perilune models in either direction.

Two configurations of the v5 networks appear in this report, and they play different roles in the project. The **raw policy** (no search) was the deployed product at the time of the study — the configuration the site's players faced — and producing the strongest possible raw network is the project's overall goal. This deployment-fidelity claim is checkable, not asserted: the model the site serves (`hearts_web_model.pth`) has md5 8a89da90d522fe51dff4ae2fc8170961 — the lineage id *is* that file's md5 — and is byte-identical to the archived milestone checkpoint of the same lineage; the study's traced network matches it parameter-for-parameter (91/91 tensors within 1e-6). The serving code loads it CPU-only by design and selects moves by raw argmax with no search (the equity net is used there only to display win probabilities). Study 2's raw-on-CPU configuration is therefore the served product exactly. The **searched configuration** (K = 64 determinizations, 256 in endgame states, pass search k = 24) is the default *teacher* setting — the configuration that generates the raw net's training targets — and ships as a released model that players can run against on their own hardware. Neither configuration was tuned for this study.

The arena is xinxin's own harness (his `HeartsCardGame` match loop, his rules engine, his move generator as the legality authority), with Perilune wired in through a bridge that reconstructs each deal into Perilune's environment and cross-checks hand, seat, and legality on every decision, aborting on any mismatch. GPL/MIT separation confines the shared arena to his harness by design; the bridge code is GPL-derived and is published in a separate GPLv3 repository (`perilune-xinxin-eval`).

## 2. Preregistered design (Study 1)

*Study 2's design is described in §S2.1; the shared machinery (complement pairs, CRN, verification, isolation, the frozen analyzer/verifier/supplement) is as below.*

**Unit of analysis: the complement pair.** Each seed is played twice with engine seat assignment flipped; dealt hands are identical across the two sides (verified per deal by an independent third implementation of the rules), so deal luck cancels within the pair. Compositions are stratified and never pooled: 2v2 (six seatings, the symmetric headline) and 1v3+3v1 (the challenger view, registered secondary at half n).

**Arms** (all complement-paired, one shared seed block `30260814 + 7919·i`, disjoint from the pilot; deals identical across arms by CRN):
- **M-A** — both engines at shipped defaults (Perilune K=64/256, pass-k 24; xinxin sims=10,000, worlds=30, ε=0.1, OM-2). 24 pairs 2v2 + 12 pairs 1v3+3v1.
- **M-B** — no search on either side (Perilune raw sequential policy; xinxin's side is `HeartsPlayout::DoMinPlay` at ε = 0 for card play plus his `selectPassCards` passing heuristic — a floor-setting construction, characterized honestly in §3.2). Same n.
- **M-C** — xinxin at sims=93,000 (the wall-clock-parity point calibrated 2026-08-13, re-verified immediately before the run; see §5) vs. default Perilune. 12 pairs 2v2, registered as directional (powered for d = 0.5).
- **D-axis** — pure deal skill: 156 complement-paired single deals at M-A settings, pass direction rotated across pairs, start-of-match context.
- **Sweep** — xinxin at x ∈ {300, 1k, 3k, 10k, 30k, 93k} sims vs. Perilune **raw**, 8 pairs per level, same seeds at every level (CRN); registered as estimation only.

**Metrics** (frozen `analyze.py`, md5 200ad385…; supplementary registered statistics in `supplement_stats.py`, md5 00f0f084…, written and frozen pre-unblinding): primary = paired mean-placement difference (tie-averaged), co-primary = paired match-win difference (tie-split), Holm over the two per arm/stratum; robustness = exact Wilcoxon signed-rank and exact sign test; secondaries (never gating) with 95% t CIs. Sign convention: negative placement/score favours Perilune; positive win difference favours Perilune.

**Sample sizes** were set by a mechanical rule from a 12-pair variance pilot (SD of paired placement = 0.555 → n = 24; MDE at n = 24 is 0.317 places at 80% power; at n = 12, 0.449). The pilot's pairs are excluded from all arms and its effect direction is registered as non-evidence.

**Verification.** Every arm's full-deal log (games.jsonl: dealt hands, passes, all 52 plays, scores) was checked by an independent verifier (third implementation of the rules — partition of the deck, pass consistency, follow-suit legality, trick winners, score recomputation, totals accumulation, complement-hand identity). **Every counted arm verified with 0 hard errors and 0 logger error records.** Any bridge FATAL or verifier hard error voids the arm by rule.

**Information isolation.** Because the bridge replays each deal into Perilune's environment — which necessarily contains all four hands — a skeptical reader should ask whether Perilune's *decision path* ever reads the three hidden hands. Structurally it cannot: the policy input is built by `ObserveFor(seat)` (the same imperfect-information observation used in all training), and the search's determinizations are sampled from public context only (own hand, the played set, opponents' hand *sizes*, and voids inferred from play). We tested this directly with a permutation probe: decision states constructed twice with identical acting hand, identical public history, and identical engine seeds, differing only in how the unseen cards are distributed among the hidden seats. Across six states in both engine modes, decisions were **identical in 12/12 comparisons** under hidden-hand permutation, while a sensitivity control that perturbed the *acting* hand instead changed the decision in 9/12 — the harness detects input differences, and the hidden layout is not an input. The probe ships with the evaluation code and is part of the pre-flight battery.

**Multiplicity scope.** In Study 1, Holm correction is applied over the two primaries *within* each arm/stratum, as registered; there is no registered family-wide control across its ~six cells (twelve primary tests). For transparency we computed a family-wide Holm over all twelve: every Study-1 finding reported as significant survives it (the largest family-adjusted p, the 1v3+3v1 win primary, is ≈ 0.028). Study 2 has exactly one claim-carrying cell (two tests, Holm m = 2; §S2.1), so no family question arises there.

**Registered vs. realized power.** The pilot's paired SD (0.555) underestimated the realized paired SDs of the arms — 0.774 (M-A 2v2), 0.99 (M-A 1v3+3v1), 0.849 (M-C) — by roughly 40–80%, so every arm is somewhat less powered than its registered projection. Realized MDEs (2.80 × realized SE, 80% power, two-sided α = 0.05): M-A 2v2 **0.44** places (registered projection 0.317), M-A 1v3+3v1 **0.80**, M-B 2v2 **0.18**, M-C **0.69** (projection 0.449), D-axis **0.17** points-scale placement. All MDE-bounded statements in this report use the realized values; the M-A headline effect (0.66) exceeds even its realized MDE.

### 3.2 Policy-only (M-B): a floor-setting ablation, honestly labeled

**What the opponent is.** This arm's xinxin side is not a player from his program. Its card play is `HeartsPlayout::DoMinPlay` — the deliberately cheap policy his UCT uses to *finish rollouts inside simulations*, which never chooses a real move in his program — run here at ε = 0 (no random-move noise; the ε = 0.1 his search uses applies to playouts inside M-A's simulations, not to this arm). DoMinPlay plays **at random when leading and when following suit** (a reservoir pick over his reduced move set — one that starts at the second list element, so the first-listed move can never be chosen when two or more exist: uniform over the tail, not the set) and, when sloughing, **dumps points**: it takes Q♠ if it can, otherwise a heart in preference to anything else. It is a point-dumping rollout policy, which is exactly what one wants inside a UCT rollout and exactly what saturates a scale in real play. Passing uses his real player's `selectPassCards` heuristic. The pairing is our construction (registered as exactly this in the prereg, so the arm ran as registered — but "xinxin without search" would overstate what it is). His repo does ship genuine standalone non-search players — `HeartsCardPlayer` (with a hand evaluator and move ranker) and `HeartsDucker` — which this study did not use; a real "neural policy vs. hand-built heuristics" comparison is future work (§8).

**What the arm shows.** 2v2, n = 24 pairs: placement **−1.885** (CI [−2.02, −1.75]; −2 is the theoretical maximum), score −63.9/match, wins +1.79/pair, 45.5 of 48 matches; the 1v3+3v1 stratum matches (−1.83; the lone raw-policy Perilune won 12/12). Against an opponent that is near-random in two of three play situations, almost any competent player would score near the boundary — **the scale saturates, so this measurement has little discriminative power about Perilune's strength**. Its registered role is narrower and it fills it: with zero playouts on either side the arm sets the floor of the budget bracket and confirms the raw policy is a competent hearts player standing alone. It says nothing about search, and the product-vs-his-standard-configuration question is answered by Study 2 (§S2), not here.

The crossing "price" of the raw net is at least ~1,000 sims and may exceed 93,000.

**Reconciling the sweep's top level with M-C.** At first glance these two results seem to be in tension: xinxin at 93k reaches parity with *searched* Perilune (M-C) yet fails to overtake the *raw* net (sweep) — shouldn't beating the weaker variant be easier? The resolution has two parts. First, on the placement axis nothing is actually ordered: the two estimates (−0.083, CI [−0.62, +0.46] and −0.125, CI [−0.61, +0.36]) differ by 0.04 places with ~±0.5 of uncertainty each, so the data resolve no placement ordering among {searched Perilune, raw Perilune, xinxin@93k}. Second, the ordering that *is* visible sits on the win axis, and it is coherent: against the same 93k opponent on the same deals (CRN), the raw net's win difference is −0.75/pair while the searched engine's is +0.50/pair — a ~1.25 wins/pair swing from adding search. This is the champion's documented objective at work: the match-aware search does not buy mean placement, it *spends* placement to buy P(place 1). So xinxin@93k does overtake the raw net in the currency his search optimizes (wins, point estimate), while the searched engine claws that margin back; on placement all three remain within noise. (Comparisons against different opponents need not chain in any case — engine styles differ, and strategy strength is not guaranteed transitive.)

### 3.7 Grounding the baselines: whose defaults, and what they cost

Both studies compare against xinxin at his shipped default and treat Perilune's two configurations as given, so both baselines need defending. (Historical note: Study 1's registered interpretation scoped its headline to "practical budgets" — the searched engine's advantage was undetectable at extreme xinxin budgets, §3.3 — and this section originally existed to defend that scoping. Study 2 measured the raw net across the whole tested budget range and made its cost comparison same-silicon, so the combined headline no longer needs the scoping phrase; the evidence below now serves the headline directly.)

**xinxin's side.** sims = 10,000 / worlds = 30 / C = 0.4 / opponent-model level 2 / ε = 0.1 / threaded is not our choice of handicap — it is the author's shipped primary player, verbatim (`main.cpp`, player type 0; his comment recording that the ε = 0 variant lost to the ε = 0.1 player sits on the ε = 0 case). Two honest qualifications on the *rules* and the *published budgets*, both of which a careful reader will find:
- The rules mask is *not* his shipped rule set: his `main.cpp` sets `kQueenBreaksHearts`, and our mask 0xda1 drops it (his 0xfa1 → our 0xda1) to match the variant Perilune was trained on. The player is verbatim; the rules are Perilune's variant. His search is rules-generic, so the effect is almost certainly small, but the asymmetry is one-directional (Perilune trained on exactly this variant; xinxin was developed under his own) — recorded in §5.
- His ICGA 2008 hearts budgets are quoted verbatim-correctly (quality-of-play "against UCT with 5000 playouts", stable "up to 50,000 playouts per turn", moon defense at 50,000, doubling worth "4 to 5 points"), but two contextual facts qualify any "inside his published range" reading. First, those quality-of-play numbers are *perfect-information* results (the paper states it shows only PI results, having never seen a strong PI player do worse under determinization). Second, and more pointed: they are **per-tree** budgets, while his driver constructs `UCT(sims/worlds)` — sims = 10,000 over 30 worlds is **333 playouts per determinized tree**, and even 93,000 gives ~3,100/tree, below his published 5,000. On the *total-playouts* reading, our M-A baseline (10,000 nominal, ~8,050 realized) sits inside his published 5,000–50,000 range toward its lower end, and our sweep (300–93,000) spans 0.006×–1.9× his largest (1.4× on realized playouts); on the *per-tree* reading, every cell we ran is shallower than anything he published. The load-bearing fact is not the range placement either way — it is that 10,000 is his shipped default, which is unassailable. We use the total-playouts reading because it is what his shipped player actually spends per decision — but state both so the choice is visible. And 93,000 was never claimed to be a fielded configuration — it was derived purely as the wall-clock-parity point against Perilune on this machine.

**Perilune's side.** Neither Perilune v5 configuration was tuned for this study, but they carry different weight (§1): the **raw policy was the deployed product at the time of the study** — the site's players faced it directly — while K = 64/256 with pass-k 24 is the pre-existing default of the *teacher* (the configuration that generates the raw net's training targets, also released for self-hosting). The wall-clock asymmetry at M-A's defaults runs *against* Perilune being the cheap side: the teacher spends ~500 ms/decision to xinxin's 40.9 ms — roughly 12× more. Neither headline claim rests on that asymmetry favouring Perilune: (i) at matched wall-clock (M-C — where on run-day measurement xinxin at 93k actually consumed *less* time per decision than the teacher while taking 192× the nominal playouts), the result is a bounded null, not a reversal — so the teacher's M-A advantage is not simply purchased with GPU time it could not afford at parity; and (ii) the raw net's claim (Study 2) involves no asymmetry at all — it runs on the same CPU as xinxin at one-sixth of his decision time per deal, and it is never clearly overtaken on placement anywhere in the tested range (§S2.3). In short: the teacher configuration is stronger at both programs' shipped defaults; the raw net is stronger on placement and equal on wins against xinxin's default while being cheaper on the same processor; and the extreme-budget regime is explicitly bounded for the searched engine (95% CI [−0.62, +0.46] places; realized MDE 0.69 — §3.3) and right-censored for the raw net's placement advantage (§S2.3) rather than claimed.

One honesty note on Study 1's "matched wall-clock" (M-C): any cost parity between a GPU-dominated engine and a CPU-dominated engine is an exchange-rate *convention*, not a measurement — milliseconds on this machine's RTX 4090 and milliseconds across this machine's 16 CPU threads are different goods priced equal by fiat. The convention is disclosed rather than hidden (this machine defines the clock, in the tradition of computer-chess time controls). It applies only to Study 1's searched-engine comparisons; Study 2's raw-net comparison is same-silicon and involves no such convention. §8 item 1 (the Perilune-side ladder) is designed to make the searched engine's conclusions independent of it wherever dominance holds.

### 3.8 Budgets — both currencies, with units and denominators made explicit

Playout counters are instrumented in both engines and are load-independent. Three facts a reader needs before using the table:

**The units are not comparable across engines.** A xinxin "playout" is one UCT iteration: a tree descent plus an ε-greedy rollout to the end of the deal, on CPU. A Perilune "playout" is one full-deal rollout of one candidate action under one sampled determinization, with policy-net move selection and an equity-net leaf evaluation, on GPU. Perilune's per-decision count is consistent with **K × |legal actions|** — the search shares its K determinizations across every candidate action, so the true per-action counts it records sum to |legal| × K (the counter reads the search's own per-action counts, not an estimate; the composition is our inference about what those counts sum to, and it fits). The table's own rows give the derivation. The **D-axis is the clean measurement**: its deals run at start-of-match context (all totals zero), and the K = 256 endgame boost fires only when some seat's match total has reached 85 — so the boost *cannot* fire there, and 229/64 = **3.58 legal actions per play decision** is the bare branching factor (an independent legality reconstruction over all 8,112 D-axis Perilune play decisions gives 3.79 — same neighbourhood). The match arms' higher means are that base raised by the boost firing on late deals: from mean = 64 · 3.58 · (1 + 3f), the implied boosted-decision fraction f is **15% (M-A 1v3), 18% (M-A 2v2), 19% (M-C)** — three independent arms landing together, at about the share of decisions one expects to fall in deals where someone has already passed 85 in a ~10-deal match to 100. The same caution the report applies to wall-clock parity (§3.7) applies here: cross-engine playout ratios are **nominal counts of non-comparable units**, an exchange-rate convention, not a compute measurement. What the M-C conclusion actually rests on is sign stability under a budget far beyond anything the program's author fields — not on any specific ratio.

**The denominators differ.** Perilune's counter covers **play decisions only** (exactly 13 × deals × Perilune seats in every arm), while xinxin's covers **play + pass decisions** (his passing runs through his search; his counts exceed the play-only figure by exactly 3 per passing seat-deal — e.g., D-axis: 9,516 = 8,112 play + 3 × 468 pass). For M-C, the nominal ratio is therefore a bracket: **192×** on xinxin's mixed denominator to **227×** if all his playouts are attributed to play decisions.

**Perilune's pass search is a real cost the playout counter omits.** Pass decisions (pass-k = 24) are timed separately — median 609 ms per pass decision in M-A 2v2 (IQR 543–683, range 410–996 across engine-matches; ~3 per passing deal per Perilune seat) — but their rollouts are not included in Perilune's playout figures. Wall-clock totals include them; the playout column does not.

### S2.4 Moons — the most pointed finding in Study 2 (descriptive only; no test registered)

xinxin shot the moon in **1,168 of 7,898 deals against the raw net at his default — 14.8% of deals, one every seven** — versus 51 for the raw net (0.6%). Three observations make this more than a curiosity:

- **The teacher's search halves his moon rate — shown within Study 1, on one seed block.** Study 1's sweep ran the raw net against xinxin@10,000 on the *same* registered CRN block as M-A, so the cleanest teacher-vs-raw comparison is same-study, same-block, same-opponent-setting: xinxin shot in 7.3% of deals against the searched teacher (36/495, M-A 2v2) and 15.7% against the raw net (24/153, sweep 10k) — a 2.2× ratio. *Provenance note:* this rate comparison was computed from Study 1's frozen logs at Study-2 review time, because Study 2 made it interesting; it is descriptive, uses no new data, and is not part of the frozen Study-1 record (§3.6 reports the same arms' moon *counts* as they were frozen; the rates and ratio are new arithmetic on them). Study 2's 14.8% on its own block reproduces the raw-net figure (the cross-study comparison, 14.8% vs. 7.3%, is exploratory by PREREG2 §6 and gives the same 2× answer).
- **The leak replicates across two studies and two seed blocks, and is flat across the whole budget range.** Study 2: 14.7% at 300 sims, 15.3% at 1k, 14.9% at 3k, 14.8% at 10k, 14.9% at 30k, 16.9% at 93k. Study 1's sweep, independently, on a different block: 11.5–17.1% across the same six levels. A 300× increase in his search budget does not change how often he shoots. That flatness says the moon rate is a property of the *raw net's defense*, not of his search strength — he does not need search to find these moons; the raw net leaves them open.
- **What a conceded moon costs in the report's own units.** The metrics here are seat differentials; on a xinxin-moon deal his shooter takes 0 and his partner 26, so the raw net's seats concede exactly **+13 points of seat differential** per moon (not 26 — the partner's 26 sits on the other side of the ledger). *Unregistered descriptive decomposition, offered as context, not as a claim:* in the confirmatory cell the raw net's per-deal seat-points margin is −3.15 on the 84.6% of deals with no moon, +13.00 on xinxin's 14.8% of moon deals, and −13.00 on its own 0.6%; the all-deals mean is −0.82. Conditioning on an outcome after the fact is exactly what the registered §8-item-3 moon tiers exist to do properly, so this is stated once here, labeled, and not built on.

Read together with §S2.2, this reframes the confirmatory result: the deployed product beats his shipped default on placement and on points *while conceding a moon every seven deals*, and the moon leak absorbs roughly three-quarters of its no-moon per-deal margin. The raw net is winning on placement despite, not because of, its moon defense. The inference — that closing the moon hole is where the remaining margin lives, and that the teacher's search already demonstrates about half of it is recoverable — is exactly where the project's attention goes next. It stays an inference: no moon test was registered in either study, and the two-tier moon analysis (§8 item 3) is the registered route to a claim.

## S3. Prior work against the same opponent: Rebstock et al. (2024) — added after author correspondence

*This paper was brought to our attention after both studies were complete and unblinded, by Nathan Sturtevant (xinxin’s author and a co-author of that paper, whose first author is Douglas Rebstock) when we contacted him before releasing this report. We could not reach the authors regarding availability of the GO-MCTS code or model, and we found no public release; its findings are included as reported. No endorsement by the authors is implied. This section reports no new data of ours; it places our numbers next to the one prior published evaluation against xinxin. Nothing here changes any registered claim, and the Study-2 stop clause forbids running new cells for this report.*

**What they did.** Rebstock, Solinas, Sturtevant & Buro (2024) introduce GO-MCTS — Monte Carlo tree search in *observation space*, where opponents’ moves are sampled from a small GPT-2 (8 layers, d = 256) trained to predict the agent’s own next observation token, with a categorical head over final point allocations as the value; no determinization, hence no strategy fusion. The transformer is trained from 4M uniform-random playouts followed by ten iterations of population self-play (500k hands each). Their non-search player, **ArgmaxVal\***, is a one-step lookahead on that model — append each legal action the model gives ≥ 5% probability, read the outcome head, take the argmax of expected point differential — and is the closest analogue to our raw net, though not the same kind of object (value-greedy over a generative model, not an imitation policy). Hearts ran in OpenSpiel (Lanctot et al. 2019), which carries a port of the same xinxin source; the tournament unit was the *hand* — 3,000 deals under all 14 mixed seatings — with significance by Wilcoxon signed-rank (p < 0.001; no interval estimates are reported).

**Their xinxin was not at the shipped default.** They set it to “2000 runs, 50 worlds, C = 0.4 — the default highest setting in its implementation.” OpenSpiel’s `XinxinBot` passes those two numbers straight to `UCT(runs, C)` and `iiMonteCarlo(uct, worlds)`, and `main.cpp` in the pinned source carries the commented arrays `sims = {1000, 10000, 100000}`, `worlds = {20, 30, 50}` (lines 12–13) — so their baseline is the top entry: **100,000 playouts per decision over 50 worlds** (2,000 per determinized tree), about 10× the shipped default that is our primary cell, and just above our 93k cells (30 worlds, ~3,100 per tree). It ran single-threaded at ~2.9 s/turn.

**Their results and ours, side by side.** Points per deal against xinxin; negative favours the neural player. Their moon rule scores the shooter −26 and the others 0, ours 0/26/26/26 — the shooter-vs-each-opponent *differential* is 26 in both, so this metric is comparable on that axis. Everything else differs — harness and rules variant, per-hand vs. match context, machine, and their reporting carries no CIs — so this is a juxtaposition across papers, not a measurement:

| player | xinxin budget | context | points/deal | cost per decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rebstock ArgmaxVal\* (no search) | 100k / 50 worlds | single hands | **−0.95** (p < 0.001) | 71 ms (single thread) |
| Rebstock GO-MCTS | 100k / 50 worlds | single hands | **−1.74** (p < 0.001) | 25.6 s |
| Perilune v5 raw (Study 2, x = 93k) | 93k / 30 worlds | match, n = 96 | −0.09 (SE 0.35) | 4.4 ms (CPU) |

Read plainly: **at the ~100k budget they tested, their reported margins exceed ours, and ours there are nulls.** Perilune v5’s advantage is at xinxin’s shipped default, a cell they did not run; the extreme-budget regime is exactly where this report’s own claims are bounded (§3.3, §S2.3). By its authors’ measurement GO-MCTS is a stronger hearts player than either configuration evaluated here against this opponent, and ArgmaxVal\* is plausibly stronger than our raw net — under the caveats above. We found no public release of the GO-MCTS code or its trained transformer and could not reach the authors on availability, so it is not currently usable as a second opponent (§8 item 7).

**What this does and does not change.** It touches no registered claim: every claim in this report was always “versus this program at these settings.” It sharpens two things a reader deserves to have stated rather than discover: (i) the “strongest classical open-source engine” framing in §1 stands — and is now supported by its author’s own group’s choice of baseline — but the qualifier *classical* is doing real work, since a stronger neural approach exists in the literature; and (ii) the successor-evaluation commitments (§8) now have a concrete external reference cell: one arm at exactly their configuration (OpenSpiel rules, xinxin at 100k / 50 worlds, per-hand scoring) would turn the table above into a real comparison, and OpenSpiel’s port is a cleaner arena for it than the GPL harness used here.

## 5. Threats to validity

- **Single opponent.** All claims are "vs. this program at these settings" — one implementation of PIMC+UCT with opponent modeling, as its author maintains it. They say nothing about the ceiling of that method family, and a stronger opponent is not hypothetical: GO-MCTS (Rebstock et al. 2024) beats xinxin at 100k playouts by 1.74 points/hand by its authors’ measurement, where our cells at a comparable budget are nulls (§S3). It is not publicly available to bridge; information-set MCTS and other neural-search hybrids remain further untested opponents.
- **Single machine; wall-clock parity drift; hardware-class exchange rate.** The 93k parity point was calibrated at ~436 ms/decision for both engines on 2026-08-13. Re-verification immediately before M-C measured Perilune at 250–609 ms/decision across four uniform-table seats (mean ≈ 418 ms; single-deal, 13 decisions/seat, load average still decaying from the previous arm — noisy) and xinxin at 254/329/424 ms for 70k/93k/120k sims: on that day 93k ≈ 0.79× Perilune's mean wall-clock and 120k ≈ parity. M-C ran at the *registered* 93,000; wall-clock is reported as measured, with this drift disclosed. The playout counts are load-independent but are **within-engine accounting, not a cross-engine currency** (§3.8: non-comparable units, different denominators) — the M-C conclusion rests on the bracketing design (a budget far beyond anything the author fields failed to reverse the sign), not on the nominal 192×–227× ratio. Beyond drift, wall-clock parity between these engines prices GPU time (Perilune) against 16-thread CPU time (xinxin) at a machine-defined exchange rate (§3.7); a different CPU/GPU pairing would move every parity point.
- **Objective mismatch.** Perilune's teacher optimizes P(place 1) in match context and measurably trades mean placement for wins; the raw net distilled from it plays a point-minimizing line; xinxin minimizes points. Two-axis reporting (placement and wins as co-primaries, plus the deal-skill axis) is the registered mitigation. In Study 1 the teacher leads on every axis, so the mismatch does not carry that result; in Study 2 the mismatch is *visible* — the raw net leads on placement and ties on wins, and the frontier's two curves cross at different budgets (§S2.3) — and is reported as such rather than resolved.
- **Opponent-model mismatch, both directions.** His OM-2 assumes xinxin-like opponents; Perilune's belief net was trained on self-play. Inherent to any cross-engine match; approximately symmetric in Study 1. In Study 2 his OM-2 faced a raw policy net in every cell — a mismatch that could cut either way and is not measured.
- **Rules-set divergence, two kinds.** (a) The arena ran Perilune's training variant, not xinxin's shipped rule set: his `main.cpp` enables `kQueenBreaksHearts` and our registered mask 0xda1 drops it (§1, §3.7). His search is rules-generic and the difference touches only when hearts may be led, so the effect is very likely small — but it is one-directional (Perilune trained under exactly these rules; xinxin was developed under his own) and it is disclosed here rather than left to be found. (b) One rare-state legality divergence within the shared rules was found and neutralized (amendment 2); the arena's rules governed. His in-search passing vs. his `selectPassCards` heuristic in M-B is a registered asymmetry of that arm. His ε = 0.1 playout noise is part of his agent *in the search arms* (it randomizes playouts inside his simulations); the M-B rollout-policy opponent ran at ε = 0, so no direct random-move noise entered that arm's real play (§3.2).
- **Dealer repair.** Amendment 3 replaced the harness's drifting shuffle counter with explicit per-deal seeds. Deals remain uniform random deals; nothing about the fix favours either engine; it restores the registered design properties (within-pair identity, cross-arm CRN).
- **GPL/MIT separation** constrains the shared arena to his harness (by design, documented); the inverse experiment (xinxin inside Perilune's harness) was considered and rejected for scope.
- **Nondeterminism.** His UCT is threaded and GPU inference is not bit-stable: runs reproduce statistically, not bit-exactly. The released logs are the record of these runs; the explicit-seed dealer makes the *deals* exactly reproducible.
- **Study 2 specifics.** Same-silicon cost comparison (both engines on the CPU) removes the hardware-class exchange rate for Study 2's dominance claim, but every cost figure remains a property of this CPU. Study 1's raw arms ran the raw net on GPU and Study 2 on CPU; strength is treated as device-independent (same weights, same argmax rule; float rounding can flip rare near-ties) — cross-study raw comparisons carry this note. During matches the raw net's CPU inference shares cores with xinxin's threaded search; this affects neither engine's strength (his playout count is fixed by sims, our argmax by weights) nor the calibration-derived cost figures, which come from isolated runs. n_conf's planning values (θ_plan = +0.15, SD UCL 1.49) were conservative — the realized SE (0.043) implies the study was substantially overpowered against its registered alternative, the correct direction of error for a confirmatory product claim. The confirmatory cell is the one where Perilune plays its cheapest configuration; matched-cost engine-design questions are Future Work by design (§8 item 1).

## 7. Deferred exploratory analyses

The exploratory battery registered with Study 1 (search-judged decision quality, belief-calibration vs. a foreign opponent, moon microscopy, equity trajectories) operates offline on the released logs of both studies, is labeled exploratory, and never supports the headline claims (evaluator bias — Perilune judging both sides — is inherent and will be stated wherever used). ## 8. Future work and open questions

Sketched here per convention; any of these that runs becomes its own preregistration first (mechanical n rules from the SDs now measured in these studies, complement pairs, fresh CRN seed blocks — never pooled with existing arms — the pre-flight configuration battery, frozen instruments). Study 2's stop clause (§S2.1) applies: none of these gate this report. In order of value:

1. **The Perilune-side cost–strength ladder against xinxin's standard configuration.** Study 2 answered the *product* half of the original item 1 (raw vs. xinxin@10k, now settled at n = 438: stronger on placement, equal on wins, one-sixth the time). The *engine-design* half remains open: how does the searched engine's advantage scale with its own budget at fixed xinxin@10k — a K-ladder {2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64} with pass search held fixed, plus a rung isolating pass search, and one deterministic equal-time rung (the most expensive bundle whose calibrated per-play-decision cost stays under xinxin's ~42 ms — an optimization output, not an experimenter choice, and no selection bracket). This was Study 2's v2 design before the raw-only pivot; it was dropped for powering reasons, not disavowed. Wall-clock parity between GPU-run search and 16-thread CPU search remains an exchange-rate convention (§3.7); dominance in every measured currency is the readout that makes conclusions currency-free where it holds. Cost per rung must be measured as distributions over dozens of deals — never a single-deal mean (§5's recal spread is the cautionary tale) — with bundles measured directly rather than extrapolated from K=64.
2. **A powered "compute excuse" study (M-C′).** The M-C null is the loosest bound in either study (realized MDE 0.69 places at n = 12; §3.3). With the paired SD now measured (≈ 0.85), n ≈ 63 pairs resolves d = 0.30 and n ≈ 91 resolves d = 0.25 at 80% power (one to two nights). Fresh seeds, new prereg, no pooling — extending the existing arm after observing its null would be data-dependent optional stopping. Study 2's lesson applies: power for the registered *conclusion* at the SD's upper confidence limit, not for a zero null at the point estimate. A group-sequential design with alpha spending would let it stop early on a decisive answer in either direction.
3. **Moon microscopy, retrospective first — in two tiers with different costs.** xinxin completed 1.9–3.1× more moons than the searched engine across Study 1's arms, and 16–60× more than the raw net across Study 2's cells (§S2.4 — the finding that most sharply points at where the remaining margin lives). The **descriptive tier** is genuinely free and log-only: replaying the released games.jsonl to tag moon attempts, missed blocks, counterfactual pre-ducks, fed points, and pass feeds, each conditioned on whether the moon was still alive (ducking and dumping are correct normal play; only moon-alive instances indict the defense). The **search-judged tier** — what each conceded moon cost Perilune *by its own search's judgment* (evaluator bias disclosed) — is not free: it requires running K = 64 search over every moon-relevant decision, modest but real GPU work. The project already has validated instruments for both tiers, and the published bridge exposes the needed replay-and-score primitive (it produces per-action search scores at an arbitrary arena position — the amendment-2 machinery), so this is roughly a day of adapter work rather than new instrumentation. Deferred from this report by choice: the released logs are permanent, so the analysis loses nothing by running after publication. Only if the measured cost is material does a prospective arm make sense — and prospectively selecting "moon-prone" deals is a selection-bias trap unless conditioning is fixed before any play is seen.
4. **Extending the raw-net frontier above 93k sims.** Study 2 fit both axes at proper n (§S2.3): the win crossing is located (≈1,700 sims) but the placement crossing is right-censored beyond 93k. Levels above 93k (~1 s/decision and up) price a configuration nobody fields; accepting the censoring is the honest and cheap choice, and the item is listed only for completeness.
5. **A real policy-vs-heuristics arm (M-B′).** M-B's opponent is a rollout policy and its scale saturates (§3.2), so neither study contains a genuine "neural policy vs. hand-built evaluation" comparison. His repo ships the pieces: `HeartsCardPlayer` (hand evaluator + move ranker) and `HeartsDucker` are standalone non-search players. Raw net vs. each, both sides at ~milliseconds/decision, complement pairs — cheap, and it would produce the discriminative search-free comparison M-B cannot.
6. **Opponent-model sensitivity.** One inexpensive arm varying xinxin's opponent-model setting at defaults would close the corresponding threats-to-validity item.
7. **Other opponents, and a standard arena.** The single-opponent limitation (§5) is only answerable by bridging additional engines. Two concrete routes now exist. (a) OpenSpiel (Lanctot et al. 2019) ships Hearts with configurable rules and a port of the same xinxin source; wrapping Perilune as an OpenSpiel bot would give a GPL-free arena others can reproduce in a standard framework, and one registered arm at exactly Rebstock et al.’s configuration (xinxin at 2000 runs × 50 worlds = 100k playouts, OpenSpiel rules, per-hand scoring, all 14 seatings) would make the §S3 juxtaposition a real comparison. (b) GO-MCTS / ArgmaxVal* (Rebstock et al. 2024) is the natural neural opponent, but no code or model is publicly released; if one becomes available it is the priority second opponent. ISMCTS implementations remain candidates after those.
